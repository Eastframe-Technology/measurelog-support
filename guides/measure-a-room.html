How to measure a room for flooring, paint or furniture

Short answer: draw the room, measure each wall at floor level in two places, keep the larger number, and write everything on the drawing. Then multiply length by width for the area and add the walls for the perimeter.

What you need

A tape measure, a pencil and paper. A helper makes long walls easier, and a laser measure is quick if you have one. A phone is fine for a first estimate, but use a tape for the numbers you will order from. See how accurate phone AR measuring is.

Step by step

  1. Sketch the floor plan. It doesn't have to be neat. Mark doors, windows, radiators and alcoves.
  2. Measure the length and the width. Go wall to wall at floor level, along the skirting boards (baseboards). Keep the tape straight and pull it tight.
  3. Measure again in a second place. Rooms are rarely exact rectangles. If you get 4.02 m at one end and 4.05 m at the other, use 4.05 m when you buy material.
  4. Note the extras. Measure door widths, the depth of door recesses, and any alcove or chimney breast.
  5. Pick one unit. Don't mix cm on one wall and feet on another. If you have to convert, use the length converter.
  6. Work it out. Use the formulas below, or the room calculator.

Area, for flooring and carpet

For a rectangle, multiply length by width. A room of 5 m by 4 m is 20 m². One of 12 ft 6 in by 10 ft is 12.5 × 10 = 125 ft².

For any other shape, split it into rectangles. For an L-shaped room, work out the outer rectangle and take off the missing corner. A room of 6 m by 5 m with a 2 m by 2 m corner missing is 30 − 4 = 26 m². Add alcoves and door recesses as small rectangles of their own.

Add some extra for cuts and mistakes. A common rule of thumb is 5 to 10%, and more for diagonal layouts or patterns that must match. Flooring comes in boxes and carpet comes in fixed widths, so the amount you buy depends on the product. Give your drawing to the seller and follow their number.

Perimeter, for baseboards and trim

The perimeter is the total length of the walls. For a rectangle, it is 2 × (length + width). The 5 m by 4 m room has 18 m of wall. Take off door openings: with one 80 cm door, you would need about 17.2 m of baseboard. Boards come in fixed lengths, and you lose some at each corner, so round up.

Wall area, for paint

Multiply the perimeter by the ceiling height. The same room with a 2.5 m ceiling has 18 × 2.5 = 45 m² of wall. Take off large windows and doors if you like. Then read the coverage on the paint can, and remember that most jobs need two coats.

For furniture

Floor area matters less than the path in. Before you buy something big, measure:

  • the width and height of each door it must pass through, with the door open;
  • the width of hallways and stairs, and any tight turn;
  • the wall space, minus radiators, sockets you need to reach, and room for doors and drawers to open.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Measuring at waist height. Walls often lean or bow, and floor level is what counts for flooring.
  • Measuring above the baseboard for wall space. A cabinet against a wall sits against the baseboard, so measure there.
  • Trusting one reading. One careless number can cost a box of flooring. Check each one.
  • Rounding down. Always round up when you are buying material.
